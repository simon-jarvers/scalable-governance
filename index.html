AI is developed and deployed faster than governance can follow.

Scalable Governance is an applied research initiative with the goal of scaling AI governance to the pace of rapidly increasing capability and volume of AI systems, without losing the human judgment and accountability that good governance requires.

We are based at the TUM Professorship of Societal Computing.

Why governance has to scale

  1. Our argument

    AI is built faster

    We argue that AI coding agents write a growing amount of the code of AI systems. Teams build faster, and more AI systems reach the market.

  2. Our argument

    Governance has to keep pace

    AI governance across all levels has to keep up with this pace. How is largely unsolved, and we argue that neglecting it poses a systemic risk.

  3. Our aim

    What we build

    An Agentic Governance Workflow: agent skills that enable effective and accountable human-AI collaboration for governance work. We research AI system developers empirically to enable evidence-based AI regulation.

By AI governance we mean the rules, processes and controls that make sure AI systems are safe and compliant. AI governance can be understood on different levels:

  • Organisation: Companies that develop and deploy AI systems turn regulatory requirements into policies and controls that impact the AI systems.
  • Verification: Auditors, notified bodies and authorities check conformity against technical standards and legal obligations.
  • Regulation: Legislators and policy-makers write AI regulations and keep them up to date.

Most of our research so far studies the organisational level.

Research

We study AI governance inside the organisations that build AI systems, and among the consultants, auditors and standard-setters who interpret the rules for them. Our most recent work focuses on making governance scale: methods and agentic tools that embed regulatory requirements in AI development workflows.

ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT 2026)

Engaged AI GovernanceAddressing the Last Mile Challenge Through Internal Expert Collaboration

Simon Jarvers, Orestis Papakyriakopoulos

Abstract

Under the EU AI Act, translating AI governance requirements into software development practice remains challenging. While AI governance frameworks exist at industry and organizational levels, empirical evidence of team-level implementation is scarce. We address this “Last Mile” Challenge through insider action research embedded within an AI startup. We present a legal-text-to-action pipeline that translates EU AI Act requirements into actionable strategies through internal expert collaboration by extracting requirements from legal text, engaging practitioners in assessment and ideation, and prioritizing implementation through collective evaluation. Our analysis reveals three patterns in how practitioners perceive regulatory requirements: convergence (compliance aligns with development priorities), existing practice (current work already satisfies requirements), and disconnection (requirements perceived as administrative overhead). Based on these patterns, we discuss when governance might be treated genuinely or performatively. Practitioners prioritize requirements that serve end-users or their own development needs, but view verification-oriented requirements as box-ticking exercises. This distinction suggests a translation challenge: regulatory requirements risk superficial treatment unless practitioners understand how compliance serves system quality and user protection. Expert collaboration offers a practical mechanism for transforming governance from external imposition to shared ownership and making previously invisible governance work visible and collective.

AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES 2026) Forthcoming

Governing by ProxyHow Regulatory Intermediaries Shape EU AI Act Compliance in Practice

Simon Jarvers, Orestis Papakyriakopoulos

Abstract

The EU AI Act assigns compliance obligations to defined actor roles along the AI supply chain, but how these obligations are enacted in practice remains understudied. This paper investigates the ecosystem of regulatory intermediaries (consultants, auditors, ethics advisors, certification bodies) through which an AI system provider translates regulatory requirements into organisational practice. Drawing on participant observation and document analysis from an embedded case study at an AI startup, the analysis applies the Regulator-Intermediary-Target (RIT) model to map sixteen intermediaries and trace the compliance pressures that drive pre-enforcement intermediary engagement. Compliance artefacts across risk management, impact assessment, and technical documentation show a consistent pattern: intermediary interpretive choices construct what AI Act compliance means for the regulated organisation. Using Edelman’s legal endogeneity framework, which theorises how organisations’ private compliance constructions can feed back into the meaning of law itself, we discuss how legal requirements are managerialised into procedural deliverables and how compliance forms diffuse without evidence of governance efficacy. The paper contributes on two levels: it extends the RIT model to organisational-level AI governance, demonstrating that compliance intermediaries perform constitutive rather than facilitative functions, and it provides an early empirical signal for legal endogeneity in AI Act compliance. We encourage more guidance from the EU and argue that the compliance ecosystem deserves the same analytical and regulatory attention currently directed at the AI supply chain.

European Conference on Algorithmic Fairness (ECAF 2026), PMLR Forthcoming

Governance-Driven DevelopmentEmbedding Regulatory Requirements in AI Development Workflows

Simon Jarvers, Orestis Papakyriakopoulos

Abstract

AI governance under the EU AI Act faces a structural misallocation of effort: management-based regulation, combined with the credence-good properties of governance outcomes, pushes resource-constrained organisations toward verification-facing documentation rather than the implementation it is meant to verify. We propose Governance-Driven Development (GDD), a framework that treats AI Act obligations and the requirements of supporting standards for safe AI development as formal specifications for software development work. GDD runs on two nested action cycles. A planning cycle scopes obligations against organisational context to produce specifications. An implementation cycle converts each specification into the artefact it specifies, with traceability back to the regulatory source. AI agents perform template-driven work; humans retain interpretation and accountability. The framework is in early implementation at an AI Startup, with unresolved load-bearing questions around knowledge base architecture and obligation decomposition.

Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS 2027) Forthcoming

Human-AI Collaboration in Compliance AutomationDesigning an Agentic Knowledge Base for AI Governance

Simon Jarvers, Carolin Röthemeier, Holger Wittges, Orestis Papakyriakopoulos

Abstract

AI governance work draws on a living body of organizational knowledge, policies, and system facts that drift and go stale as organizational and regulatory contexts change. For resource-constrained organizations under the EU AI Act and related standards, maintaining this knowledge consumes scarce compliance capacity. We applied a pattern drawn from practice, in which an LLM agent maintains a structured, citation-anchored knowledge base for AI governance. Through a Design Science Research study at an AI startup, we apply the artifact to two ISO management system standards. The artifact separates a human-authoritative source layer from an agent-moderated knowledge layer in the company’s internal governance work processes. We abstract two design principles, human authority over agent moderation and traceability, which demonstrate how to responsibly leverage advancing AI capabilities for human-agent collaboration in regulated compliance settings.

European Workshop on Algorithmic Fairness (EWAF 2025), PMLR vol. 294

Uncertainty as a Primary Barrier for Trustworthy AI Under the EU AI ActGerman SME Perspectives

Simon Jarvers*, Chiara Ullstein*, Jens Grossklags

* Both authors contributed equally.

Abstract

The European Union’s AI regulation, the EU AI Act, represents a significant shift from voluntary ethical frameworks to binding regulation, presenting implementation challenges particularly for resource-limited SMEs. Our mixed-methods research examined the EU AI Act’s impact on SMEs through surveys of German AI SMEs (N = 21) and interviews with AI SMEs and industry stakeholders (N = 13). In this extended abstract, we summarize our motivation and methods, and focus on providing results from the interviews. Our findings reveal that company size and compliance experience significantly affect estimated implementation capabilities. SMEs face considerable resource constraints across time, finances, and staffing. Implementation uncertainties – including definitional ambiguity, unclear scope, and insufficient guidance – drive strategic responses: delaying compliance efforts, modifying products to reduce regulatory burden, and frequently seeking external compliance expertise and certification. These results indicate that uncertainty emerges as the primary implementation barrier. Researchers can help reduce uncertainty by developing best-practice guidelines that support the AI Act’s trustworthy AI objectives. We conclude with recommendations for policymakers and researchers.
